\chapter{Project Vision}
\label{ch:vision}

This chapter converts the broad motivation for Manzil into a bounded project vision that can guide both implementation and evaluation. It identifies the decisions that the prototype is expected to make, the evidence needed to judge those decisions, and the conditions under which any conclusion must remain limited to simulation and selected recorded video. The chapter begins with the relationship between normal traffic control and emergency movement, then states the problem in terms that can be investigated within an undergraduate project. The evaluation plan separates perception quality, normal signal control, dynamic routing, corridor operation, and robustness so that an improvement in one area is not mistaken for success in every area. The objectives and scope retain the approved proposal while excluding forecasting, enforcement, citywide deployment, and direct operation of public signals. Constraints related to data, map quality, computing resources, and the simulation-to-reality gap are treated as design inputs rather than footnotes. Stakeholder expectations then clarify who can use the prototype and what evidence would constitute a credible project outcome.

\section{Problem Domain Overview}

Traffic-signal control allocates limited green time among movements that cannot proceed together. Fixed plans are understandable, auditable, and easy to reproduce, but they cannot respond to every short-term difference in approach demand. Adaptive control can use measured queues and waiting time, yet a controller that improves the network average may still be unacceptable if it repeatedly postpones a lightly loaded approach. Coordination adds another layer because an action at one intersection changes arrivals and queues downstream. Emergency routing operates on the same network with a stricter travel-time objective. A route is useful only when its cost reflects current link travel time, queue discharge, signal delay, and any closure that makes a movement unavailable. Signal priority may reduce ambulance stops, but an unsafe transition or poorly managed recovery can transfer excessive delay to ordinary traffic. Manzil therefore treats normal control, route selection, priority activation, passage detection, and recovery as connected decisions. It retains separate measures for the ambulance and other vehicles so that emergency benefit cannot conceal network disruption.

Figure \ref{fig:context} presents the central scope decision that separates the two evidence paths while allowing them to use the same traffic-state contract. Recorded traffic video enters the perception path, where a detector, tracker, and region-assignment process produce observable measures that can be checked against annotations. SUMO enters the controlled-experiment path and exposes exact simulated vehicle, lane, route, and signal state for repeatable controller and routing comparisons. The shared contract records the source mode, identifier, timestamp, unit, confidence, and validity of every observation. This design permits the dashboard and downstream services to consume a consistent structure without implying that the sources have equal certainty. In particular, a count or queue proxy estimated from an arbitrary camera view cannot reconstruct every lane in the selected SUMO network unless camera calibration and lane mapping have been completed. The figure is relevant because it prevents a common scope error: presenting separate perception and simulation demonstrations as though they formed a validated real-time city model. Manzil will evaluate them separately unless a later, explicitly controlled integration study becomes feasible.

\section{Problem Statement}

Within the selected prototype setting, there is no established implementation that allows the Manzil team to compare signal-control strategies and isolate the contributions of emergency routing and coordinated priority under one reproducible protocol. The project problem is therefore to design a system that observes or simulates traffic state, makes bounded control and routing decisions, and records why each decision was requested and what was actually applied. The system must support matched comparisons between fixed-time, rule-based adaptive, and reinforcement-learning control under the same demand conditions. It must also compare an unassisted ambulance, congestion-aware routing, and routing with a moving corridor without changing unrelated configuration between runs. This requires more than joining familiar technologies. Measurements must have defined units and freshness, routes must remain legal from the current vehicle position, signal changes must respect phase safety, and failed or infeasible runs must remain visible. The resulting evidence will describe performance in declared simulation and video conditions. It will not establish that the same effect would occur on public roads without calibration, controller integration, safety engineering, authority approval, and field validation.

\section{Problem Elaboration}

Five linked sub-problems determine whether the prototype can meet its evaluation objectives. First, frame-level vehicle detections must be associated over time and assigned to meaningful road regions before they can support counts, trajectories, or queue-related observations. Second, normal signal control requires a correctly configured fixed baseline and a transparent adaptive baseline before reinforcement learning can be judged fairly. Third, route costs must use compatible units and avoid adding separate congestion penalties when observed travel time already contains the same delay. Fourth, corridor control must anticipate the ambulance, clear conflicting movements, limit the duration of priority, detect passage, and restore normal service without holding the entire route green. Fifth, experiments must use matched demand, seeds, warm-up, duration, and metric definitions. These issues also interact across component boundaries. A stale observation can change an edge cost, a route revision can invalidate pending priority, and a prolonged priority phase can improve ambulance time while harming ordinary traffic. The vision consequently requires explicit freshness, single ownership of simulation time and actuation, safe fallback, route versioning, and auditable event order.

\section{Goals and Objectives}

The goal is to develop and evaluate a simulation-based intelligent traffic-management prototype that supports normal traffic control and a single emergency mission. The measurable objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=O\arabic*.]
\item Define and implement a versioned traffic-state representation shared by perception, simulation, control, routing, and visualization.
\item Evaluate a pretrained vehicle detector with multi-object tracking on held-out, source-separated traffic-video segments.
\item Construct a manageable multi-intersection SUMO network and repeatable demand scenarios.
\item Implement fixed-time and reproducible rule-adaptive controllers, followed by one feasible RL controller.
\item Compute valid ambulance routes using time-based dynamic edge costs and bounded rerouting.
\item Coordinate safe, moving signal priority and restore normal control after passage or cancellation.
\item Provide a React dashboard and FastAPI service boundary for scenario control, monitoring, and experiment comparison.
\item Record configurations and outcomes so each reported run can be reproduced and audited.
\end{enumerate}

Table \ref{tab:scope} separates the functions required for the research prototype from capabilities that would create unsupported operational or regulatory claims. The exclusions are equally important. Live government CCTV, direct operation of public signals, full-Lahore coverage, enforcement, number-plate or facial recognition, visual accident classification, forecasting, and multiple competing emergency fleets would require data, infrastructure, policy, and validation beyond the current project. OpenStreetMap supports network preparation, yet its attributes will be checked and corrected rather than assumed complete. These boundaries keep implementation feasible and ensure that the final evaluation addresses the stated objectives instead of dispersing effort across unrelated features.

\section{Sustainable Development Goals}

Manzil aligns principally with United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 11, which concerns inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable cities \cite{un2025sdg}. The relevant connection is the investigation of how existing road and signal capacity might be used more responsively under changing traffic conditions, together with a structured method for prioritizing an emergency journey. The prototype can provide evidence about simulated waiting time, queue formation, throughput, ambulance travel time, and the recovery of ordinary traffic after priority. These measures are appropriate for a computing project because they can be defined and reproduced within the selected environment. The alignment must nevertheless be stated cautiously. A SUMO experiment does not demonstrate city-level sustainability, reduce emissions directly, or establish a public-health outcome. The project also does not model patient condition, clinical triage, hospital capacity, or the relationship between travel time and survival. Its secondary relevance to health is limited to studying a transport mechanism that may reduce simulated ambulance delay. Any stronger claim would require operational traffic data, emergency-service participation, field trials, and outcome measures outside the approved scope.

\section{Constraints and Assumptions}

The main constraints are the semester-scale implementation period, limited access to calibrated local traffic data, camera perspective and occlusion, finite model-training hardware, and the gap between simulated and real driving behavior. These constraints require a network small enough for repeated runs and detailed validation. A recognizable urban extract is preferred, but a documented synthetic grid remains an acceptable fallback if imported lane connections or signal programs cannot be corrected reliably within the schedule. The core experiments assume one active ambulance, valid origin and destination edges, controlled traffic generation, and no direct actuation of public infrastructure. Video-derived queue length remains an observable proxy unless the camera is calibrated to physical road geometry. The exact tracker, reinforcement-learning algorithm, database engine, hardware, map size, timing thresholds, and training budget are proposed choices rather than confirmed facts. These assumptions will be stored with experiment configurations because they affect interpretation. When an assumption fails, the system must report the failure or use a declared fallback instead of silently changing the scenario. This treatment makes constraints part of the research design and prevents later results from appearing more general than the tested conditions support.

\section{Business and Practical Opportunity}

The practical opportunity for Manzil is an experimentation and decision-support environment rather than an immediately deployable traffic product. A repeatable simulator can help a technical team compare how signal policies react to balanced, uneven, congested, and incident-affected demand. It can also show a prospective traffic or emergency-service stakeholder why the fastest ambulance route may differ from the shortest route, how a moving corridor progresses, and how much ordinary traffic is disrupted during and after priority. The dashboard and stored run manifests make those decisions visible instead of presenting the controller as a black box. This capability could support later feasibility studies, training demonstrations, or policy prototyping if the research results justify continued work. A commercial or public-sector deployment would require substantially more evidence: calibrated local demand, validated sensing, compatible roadside controllers, resilient communications, cybersecurity controls, privacy review, safety assurance, authority approval, and field trials. The current project assumes none of these partnerships or approvals and makes no revenue claim. Its immediate value lies in producing a defensible prototype and evaluation method that can reveal both benefits and limitations.

Table \ref{tab:stakeholders} identifies the parties who influence the prototype or may later be affected by its decisions. The student team is the active system operator during development and evaluation, with responsibility for scenario configuration, execution, diagnosis, and evidence preservation. The supervisor and evaluation panel require transparent scope, reproducible methods, and a clear distinction between implemented and planned work. A prototype operator needs understandable controls and visible warnings but is not treated as an authorized emergency dispatcher. Traffic authorities and emergency services are prospective stakeholders whose operational knowledge would be necessary for any future deployment; they are not represented as current partners or as having approved the design. Road users are indirect affected parties because priority can shift delay between approaches even when an ambulance benefits. This stakeholder distinction shapes the interface and evaluation. The dashboard must expose source mode, stale data, fallback, route revisions, and corridor state to the operator, while experimental reporting must include ordinary-traffic effects for reviewers and prospective authorities. No stakeholder is assigned a role, credential, or commitment that is unsupported by the project record.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter has defined a project vision that is narrower than operational traffic management but broad enough to support a substantive research and development investigation. Manzil will connect traffic observation, comparative signal control, dynamic emergency routing, and a moving green corridor inside a controlled and auditable prototype. The two evidence paths remain separate, and the core emergency case contains one active ambulance so that route and priority behavior can be examined without inventing an untested fleet-arbitration policy. The objectives identify the controlled comparison and corresponding measures for each claim. Scope boundaries exclude live government integration, citywide deployment, forecasting, enforcement, and unsupported safety or health conclusions. Constraints concerning data, map quality, hardware, and simulation fidelity are incorporated into configuration and reporting. Stakeholder roles are limited to those supported by the project record, and credible evaluation requires legal behavior, reproducible runs, visible failure, and balanced reporting of benefits and side effects. These decisions guide the literature review in Chapter \ref{ch:literature}, which examines the research basis for each technical component and identifies the limitations that Manzil's methodology must address.
